Many businesses copy the price the next shop charges. But your costs are not the same as theirs. If your price does not cover all your costs, you can be busy every day and still lose money.

## Step 1: Find the cost of one item

Add up everything it takes to have one item ready to sell.

**If you buy and resell:** the price you paid, plus your share of transport and loading to bring it to your shop.

**If you make it:** all the ingredients or materials for a batch, plus gas or charcoal, packaging and labels. Divide by the number of items the batch makes.

**Example (shito):** one batch uses GHS 180 of pepper, oil, fish and spices, GHS 30 of gas, and 20 jars with labels at GHS 3 each (GHS 60). The batch costs GHS 270 and makes 20 jars, so one jar costs **GHS 13.50**.

## Step 2: Add your running costs

Rent, light, water, phone and your own pay also have to come from sales. A simple way: add up these costs for a month and divide by how many items you sell in a month.

**Example:** GHS 600 a month of running costs, 300 jars sold a month: about **GHS 2 per jar**. So the full cost of a jar is about GHS 15.50.

## Step 3: Add your profit

Decide how much profit you want on each item. Many small businesses aim for 20% to 50% above full cost, depending on the product and the competition.

**Example:** GHS 15.50 plus 40% is about GHS 21.70. Round to **GHS 22**.

## Step 4: Check the market

Compare with what customers will pay. If your price is much higher than others:

- look for cheaper suppliers or buy in bulk with other traders,
- reduce waste,
- or offer something extra: better packaging, delivery, a bigger size.

If your price is much lower, you may be leaving money on the table.

## Keep prices up to date

When the cost of ingredients or transport goes up, your price must follow. In this app, when you record **Buy stock**, the product's cost price updates, and the product screen shows how much you make on each one.
