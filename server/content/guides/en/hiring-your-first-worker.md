A good worker lets the business grow beyond what one person can do. Starting the relationship properly avoids misunderstandings later.

## Put the agreement in writing

Even for a relative or an apprentice, write down and both sign:

- the job and the working days and hours,
- the pay, and when it is paid,
- the start date and any trial period,
- for apprentices: what they will learn, for how long, and any fees or allowance.

Keep a copy each. Ghana's Labour Act sets out workers' rights, such as rest days, leave and notice; a Labour Department office or GEA Business Advisory Centre can explain what applies.

## SSNIT contributions

If you employ workers, you must register as an employer with **SSNIT** and pay contributions for them every month:

- the worker contributes **5.5%** of their basic salary (you deduct it from their pay),
- you as the employer add **13%**,
- **18.5%** in total.

**Example:** basic salary GHS 1,000. You deduct GHS 55 from the worker's pay and add GHS 130 yourself: GHS 185 in total is paid for that worker.

Pay by the **14th of the following month**. Late payment attracts a penalty of 3% a month on what is owed, so build it into your monthly costs. In this app, record it as an expense under **SSNIT contributions**.

## Train someone to run things

If the business stops whenever you are away, it can't grow. Start small:

1. Write down how you do the main tasks: opening, pricing, recording sales, cleaning, closing.
2. Show your worker, then watch them do it.
3. Let them run it for a day while you are reachable on the phone.

## Record sales, even when you're not there

Ask your worker to record every sale as it happens, and check the records at the end of each day. Counting stock weekly will show you if anything goes missing.

## Pay on time

Paying on the agreed day is the cheapest way to keep a good worker. Plan for it: wages, SSNIT and your own pay come before spending on anything new.
